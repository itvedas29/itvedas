Expert Creates Tool to Track Major Data Breaches While Avoiding Damage Estimates

New database helps track significant cybersecurity incidents without calculating financial fallout from breaches.

A New Way to Document Cyberattacks

Richard Bird, a veteran in the cybersecurity field, has launched a new tracking system designed to document major data breaches and security incidents. Rather than focusing on calculating the dollar amounts lost in these attacks, the resource takes a different approach by concentrating on gathering factual information about when breaches happened, what organizations were affected, and what types of data were exposed.

The initiative fills a gap that many security professionals have struggled with for years. While numerous databases exist online, they often contain conflicting information, unreliable damage estimates, or incomplete details about incidents. Bird's tool aims to provide a clearer, more consistent way for experts to research and understand the landscape of major security failures.

What This Means for Security Understanding

Think of this resource like a medical database that documents disease outbreaks without trying to guess treatment costs. The focus stays on the facts: where the problem occurred, how many people were affected, and what happened. This approach has real advantages for people who need reliable information.

  • For journalists: A trustworthy source of breach details without speculation about financial impact
  • For cybersecurity professionals: Accurate historical data to identify patterns and trends in attacks
  • For policymakers: Evidence-based information to support new regulations or security standards
  • For everyday people: Clear explanations of major incidents that might affect their personal information

The decision to avoid calculating losses is intentional and strategic. Damage estimates from breaches are notoriously unreliable. Companies may not know the full impact for months or years, insurance claims get disputed, and different sources use different math. By stepping away from this guessing game, the index provides something more valuable: honest, verifiable information.

Why You Should Care About Better Data

When breaches happen, confusion often spreads faster than facts. You might see one news outlet reporting a breach affected thousands of people while another claims millions. Different sources give wildly different estimates of financial damage. This confusion makes it harder for everyone—from security teams protecting your data to lawmakers trying to create better protections—to understand what's really happening.

A reliable tracking system helps cut through the noise. It creates a historical record that multiple parties can reference and trust. Over time, this builds a clearer picture of how cybersecurity threats are evolving and where the biggest vulnerabilities lie.

Better information leads to better decisions about protecting your personal data and digital life.

What You Can Do

If you work in security, journalism, or policy, familiarize yourself with this new resource. Bookmark it as a reference point when researching incidents. If you're an average internet user curious about your own data security, use it to understand which major breaches have occurred and whether your information might have been exposed.

The broader lesson: when choosing which sources to trust about cybersecurity threats, favor those that stick to documented facts rather than speculative financial figures.

Richard Bird's approach demonstrates that sometimes the most powerful tool for understanding security threats is simply gathering what we know with confidence and being honest about what we don't.
