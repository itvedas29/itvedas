Breaking the Mold: How an Economics Background Led to Top Security Role at Global Manufacturing Firm

Economics graduate becomes CISO, proving cybersecurity leadership doesn't require a traditional tech career path.

The Unconventional Path to Cybersecurity Leadership

A major conversation is emerging in the information security world about who gets to lead organizations' digital defense strategies. Andreas Gaetje's career at Körber AG, a multinational industrial software company, demonstrates something many aspiring professionals overlook: reaching the top security officer position doesn't require spending decades in technical programming roles.

Gaetje's journey began in economics, a field that might seem worlds away from protecting computer networks and data systems. Yet he climbed the corporate ladder to become Chief Information Security Officer—one of the most critical roles in any organization. This achievement challenges a common misconception that only hardcore technology specialists can succeed in cybersecurity leadership.

Why This Matters for the Security Industry

The cybersecurity field faces a persistent talent shortage. Organizations struggle to find qualified professionals to fill security positions, and the pressure intensifies as digital threats multiply daily. However, an overly narrow hiring philosophy—only recruiting people with specific technical backgrounds—actually shrinks the talent pool rather than expanding it.

Think of it like building a sports team. If a coach only recruits players who played the exact same sport throughout childhood, they miss talented athletes with transferable skills from other disciplines. Similarly, organizations that demand exclusive technical backgrounds eliminate potential leaders who bring different perspectives and valuable capabilities.

Gaetje's background in economics actually provides distinct advantages. Understanding business finances, operational costs, and strategic planning are essential for a CISO. These professionals must justify security spending to executive teams, manage budgets, and make decisions that balance protection with productivity. Someone trained to think economically can excel at these tasks.

What This Tells Us About Leadership

Security leadership requires more than technical knowledge. It demands:

  • Communication skills – Explaining complex threats to non-technical executives
  • Strategic thinking – Aligning security with business goals
  • Decision-making ability – Choosing between competing priorities under pressure
  • Business acumen – Understanding how security investments impact company performance
  • People management – Building and motivating security teams

These leadership qualities matter more at the CISO level than the ability to write code or configure firewalls. Those technical skills remain important for specialists on the security team, but they're different from what executive security roles require.

What This Means for Your Career

If you're considering a cybersecurity career but worry your background isn't "technical enough," Gaetje's example offers encouragement. Your existing skills—whether in business, psychology, project management, or other fields—have value in security roles. The industry needs people who can think strategically and communicate effectively, not just those who've coded since childhood.

Similarly, if you're hiring for security positions, consider candidates with diverse backgrounds. Look for strong analytical thinking, proven judgment, and the ability to learn quickly. These traits often matter more than matching a specific technical checklist.

Cybersecurity leadership is broadening, and that's ultimately good for organizations facing increasingly sophisticated threats.
